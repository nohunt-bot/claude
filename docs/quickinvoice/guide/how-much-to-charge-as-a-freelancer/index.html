How much should I charge as a freelancer?

Most freelancers guess their rate by glancing at what others charge — then quietly resent it for a year. There's a better way: work backwards from the income you actually need. Below is a simple formula, two worked examples, and the billable-hours reality check that stops you from accidentally underpricing yourself.

Start with the income you need, not the market

Your rate isn't a number you copy from a forum — it's the number that covers your salary, your costs, and the hours you can't bill. Add those up first, then translate them into an hourly rate.

Hourly rate = (Target income + Business costs) ÷ Billable hours per year

Target income — the salary you want to take home, before tax.
Business costs — software, equipment, insurance, subscriptions, accounting.
Billable hours — the hours you can actually invoice, not the hours you work.

The billable-hours reality check

This is the step that trips people up. A full-time job is roughly 2,080 hours a year, but you can't bill all of them. Admin, marketing, invoicing, sick days, holidays, and gaps between projects all eat into billable time. A realistic figure for most solo freelancers is 1,000–1,200 billable hours a year — barely half of "full time."

Hours in a work year~2,080
Minus holiday, sick & admin (~25%)~1,560
Minus marketing, sales & unbilled gaps (~25%)~1,170
Realistic billable hours~1,100

If you set your rate against 2,080 hours, you'll come up short on roughly half your income. Always divide by your billable hours.

Worked example: a $70k target

Say you want to take home $70,000 and your business costs run about $8,000 a year. Using ~1,100 billable hours:

($70,000 + $8,000) ÷ 1,100 = ~$71/hour

Round it up to a clean $75/hour to leave margin for slow months. That $75 is your floor, not your ceiling — specialised or in-demand work can go well above it.

Notice how far this sits above the "$70,000 ÷ 2,080 = $34/hour" you'd get from the naive calculation. The gap is exactly why so many freelancers feel underpaid.

Hourly vs project pricing

Once you know your hourly floor, decide how to present it. Most freelancers use both depending on the job.

ApproachBest forWatch out for
HourlyOpen-ended work, retainers, anything where scope shiftsCaps your income at the clock; clients police your hours
Per project (fixed)Well-defined deliverables you can scopeScope creep eats your margin — define what's included
Day rateConsulting, on-site work, full-day blocksHalf-days still cost you a full day's opportunity

To quote a fixed-price project, estimate the hours honestly, multiply by your hourly rate, then add a buffer of 15–20% for revisions and the inevitable surprises. As you get faster, project pricing rewards your efficiency in a way hourly billing never will.

How to raise your rates without losing clients

  • Raise on new clients first. Quote your new, higher rate to every new lead. The market tells you quickly whether it sticks — and it usually does.
  • Give existing clients notice. A short, friendly email 30–60 days ahead ("from [date], my rate will be [new rate]") is professional and rarely contested for good work.
  • Tie increases to value, not time. Anchor the conversation on results you've delivered, not on the fact that a year has passed.
  • Expect to lose your cheapest clients — that's the point. Raising rates filters out low-margin work and frees up hours for better-paying projects.

Don't forget tax and the "self-employment" premium

Your rate is pre-tax. As a freelancer you also cover the costs an employer would normally absorb — self-employment tax, your own benefits, retirement, and time off. That's why freelance rates look high next to an equivalent salary: a chunk of every invoice is funding things a paycheck would have hidden. Set money aside for tax from each payment so a clean-looking rate doesn't become a nasty April surprise.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate my freelance hourly rate?

Add the annual income you want to take home to your yearly business costs, then divide by your realistic billable hours — usually around 1,100 for a solo freelancer, not the full 2,080 in a work year. For example, ($70,000 + $8,000) ÷ 1,100 works out to about $71/hour, which you'd round up to leave margin.

Should I charge hourly or per project?

Charge hourly for open-ended or shifting work, and a fixed project price for well-defined deliverables you can scope confidently. Project pricing rewards you for working efficiently, but only if you define what's included to protect against scope creep. Many freelancers use both depending on the job.

How much should a beginner freelancer charge?

Beginners often undercharge out of fear, but the income-based formula still applies — work out the rate that covers your costs and target income, then perhaps shave a little while you build a portfolio. Resist going far below your floor; it's far easier to start at a fair rate than to claw back from a cheap one.

How often should I raise my freelance rates?

Review your rates at least once a year, and raise them on new clients immediately. For existing clients, give 30–60 days' notice and anchor the increase to the value you deliver. Expect to lose your lowest-paying clients — that's a feature, not a bug.
