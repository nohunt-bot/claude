Invoice vs receipt: what's the difference?

They look similar and people swap the words all the time, but an invoice and a receipt do opposite jobs. The short version: an invoice asks for money before it's paid; a receipt confirms money that's already changed hands. Get the two straight and your records — and your clients — stay tidy. Here's the difference in plain English, plus when to send each.

The one-line difference

An invoice is a request for payment. You send it before you've been paid, and it says "here's what you owe, and by when." A receipt is proof of payment. You send it after the money lands, and it says "this has been paid in full — thank you."

Same transaction, two different moments. The invoice opens the loop; the receipt closes it.

What goes on an invoice

An invoice is a formal request, so it carries the details a client needs to pay you correctly:

  • Your name/business and contact details, and the client's
  • A unique invoice number for your records
  • An itemised list of work or products, with amounts
  • The total due and any tax
  • Payment terms and a due date (e.g. "Net 30")
  • How to pay — bank details, payment link, etc.

What goes on a receipt

A receipt is shorter because the work is done and the money is in. It just needs to prove the payment happened:

  • The date the payment was received
  • The amount paid
  • How it was paid (card, bank transfer, cash)
  • What it was for — often a reference back to the invoice number
  • A note that the balance is now paid in full

So which do I send, and when?

The order is always the same:

  1. Finish the work (or agree the sale).
  2. Send the invoice — this is your request to be paid, with a due date.
  3. Client pays.
  4. Send a receipt — confirmation the invoice is settled.

For small freelance jobs, many clients don't ask for a separate receipt — the paid invoice plus the bank record is enough. But sending a quick "paid in full" receipt is a small, professional touch, and some clients (especially companies claiming expenses) will need one for their own books.

Why the distinction matters for your taxes

Come tax time, the two documents prove different things. Invoices show what you billed — your income on paper. Receipts (the ones you receive for things you buy) prove your business expenses. Keeping both, organised by date and number, is what turns a stressful tax season into a filing exercise. Tax authorities generally expect you to keep these records for several years, so don't bin them once a job is done.

A common mix-up: an unpaid invoice is not proof you were paid. Only a receipt — or the matching bank deposit — confirms the money actually arrived. If a payment is running late, that's a chasing problem, not a receipt problem; see how to write a late-payment reminder.

Frequently asked questions

Is an invoice the same as a receipt?

No. An invoice is a request for payment that you send before you've been paid, showing what's owed and by when. A receipt is proof of payment that you send after the money has been received. They cover the same transaction but at opposite ends — the invoice opens it, the receipt closes it.

Do I send an invoice or a receipt first?

The invoice comes first. You send the invoice to request payment, the client pays, and then you can send a receipt confirming the invoice has been paid in full. A receipt before payment doesn't make sense, because there's nothing yet to confirm.

Does a paid invoice count as a receipt?

It can, informally. If you mark an invoice "paid in full" with the date and method of payment, it serves as a receipt for many small jobs. For cleaner records — or when a client needs one for expenses — a separate, short receipt is clearer.

Do freelancers need to give receipts?

You're not always required to, but it's good practice. Some clients need a receipt to claim the cost as a business expense, and keeping your own copies makes your tax records far easier. At minimum, mark invoices as paid and keep the matching bank record.
