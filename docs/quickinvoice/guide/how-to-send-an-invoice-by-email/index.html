How to send an invoice by email

For most freelancers and small businesses, email is how invoices get sent — and a clear, professional email gets paid faster than a bare attachment with no message. Here's exactly what to write in the subject line and body, whether to attach a PDF or paste a link, and a copy-paste template you can reuse for every client.

The short version

A good invoice email does four things: it's easy to recognize, easy to act on, easy to file, and easy to pay. In practice that means:

  • Subject line: include the word "invoice", the number, and your business name.
  • Attachment: attach the invoice as a PDF, not a Word or Excel file.
  • Body: a short, friendly note with the amount, due date, and how to pay.
  • Recipient: send it to the person who actually pays — often accounts payable, not your day-to-day contact.

1. Write a clear subject line

The subject line decides whether your invoice gets opened today or buried. Make it scannable for a busy accounts inbox: state that it's an invoice, give the number, and name your business so it's easy to search for later.

Don't writeWrite instead
"Hello"Invoice #1024 from [Your Business] — due [date]
"Following up"Invoice #1024 for [project] — $[amount]
"Payment"Invoice #1024 — [Your Business], due [date]

Including the due date in the subject quietly sets the clock and removes the "I didn't know when it was due" excuse later.

2. Attach the invoice as a PDF

Always send a PDF. A PDF looks the same on every device, can't be accidentally edited, and reads as professional. Avoid sending a Word document (it can reflow or be altered) or a spreadsheet (it looks unfinished and exposes your formulas). Name the file clearly too — Invoice-1024-YourBusiness.pdf beats document(3).pdf when the client searches for it months later.

3. Write a short, friendly body

The email body shouldn't repeat the whole invoice — the PDF does that. It just needs to greet the client, point at the attachment, restate the headline numbers, and say how to pay. Five sentences is plenty.

Subject: Invoice #1024 from [Your Business] — due [date]

Hi [Name],

Thanks again for the [project] work — it was a pleasure. Please find invoice #1024 attached for [amount], due on [date].

Payment details are on the invoice; you can pay by [bank transfer / your method]. Just let me know if you need it in any other format for your accounts team.

Thanks so much,
[Your name]
[Your business] · [phone / email]

That's the whole thing. Warm, specific, and it tells the client exactly what to do next.

4. Send it to the right person

Your day-to-day contact is often not the person who pays. Before sending, ask: "Who should I send invoices to?" Many companies have a dedicated accounts-payable address. Send the invoice there and CC your main contact so they know it's gone out. Getting this right is one of the biggest reasons some freelancers get paid in days and others wait weeks.

5. Follow up if it goes quiet

If the due date passes with no payment, don't stew — send a short, polite reminder. Most late invoices are simple oversights and a friendly nudge resolves them. We have copy-paste templates for this: see how to write a late payment reminder email.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • No subject, or a vague one. "Hi" gets ignored; "Invoice #1024 — due Friday" gets opened.
  • Attaching an editable file. Word and Excel look unfinished and can be altered — send a PDF.
  • Burying the ask. State the amount, due date, and how to pay right in the body.
  • Forgetting payment details. If the client has to email you back for your bank info, you've added days.
  • Sending only to your contact. CC or address the accounts team so it reaches whoever cuts the cheque.

Frequently asked questions

Should I send an invoice as a PDF or in the email body?

Attach a PDF. A PDF renders the same everywhere, can't be accidentally edited, and looks professional. Keep the email body short — a greeting, the headline amount and due date, and how to pay — and let the attached PDF carry the line-item detail.

What should the subject line of an invoice email be?

Include the word "invoice", the invoice number, your business name, and ideally the due date — for example, "Invoice #1024 from Acme Design — due June 30". This makes it easy to spot in a busy inbox and easy to search for later.

What do I write in the body of an invoice email?

Keep it to a few sentences: greet the client, thank them or reference the work, point to the attached invoice with the amount and due date, and state how to pay. There's no need to repeat every line item — that's what the attachment is for.

Who should I send the invoice to?

Send it to whoever actually processes payments — often a dedicated accounts-payable email rather than your day-to-day contact — and CC your main contact so they know it went out. If you're unsure, ask the client who invoices should go to before you send the first one.
