How to invoice as a freelancer

Your first invoice as a freelancer is mostly about getting a few details right so you actually get paid — and paid on time. Here's exactly what to put on it, how to number and send it, and what to do when a client goes quiet.

What every freelance invoice needs

An invoice isn't a formal contract, but it is the document a client uses to pay you and to file their own books. Leave something off and you invite a delay. Include all of this:

  • The word "Invoice" — so it isn't mistaken for a quote or a receipt.
  • Your name or business name and contact details (email is enough).
  • The client's name and the contact who approves payment.
  • A unique invoice number — see below.
  • The issue date and the due date (not just "due on receipt").
  • Line items: what you did, the quantity or hours, and the rate.
  • The subtotal, any tax, and the total owed, in one currency.
  • How to pay you — bank/transfer details, PayPal, or a payment link.
  • Your tax ID if your country requires it on invoices.

Step by step

  1. Number it. Use a simple sequence like 2026-001, 2026-002. Sequential numbers keep your records clean and look professional. Never reuse a number.
  2. Describe the work plainly. "Website homepage redesign — 12 hrs @ $75" beats "Design services." Clear line items get approved faster and prompt fewer questions. Not sure what rate to put here? See how much to charge as a freelancer.
  3. Set clear payment terms. "Net 14" (due in 14 days) is a friendly default for freelancers. Put the actual due date on the invoice so there's no ambiguity.
  4. Add tax if it applies. Charge the rate your jurisdiction requires and show it as a separate line so the client can see the breakdown.
  5. Send a PDF, not an editable file. A PDF can't be altered in transit and prints consistently. Attach it to a short, polite email.
  6. Log it and follow up. Note the date you sent it. If the due date passes, send a gentle reminder.

How to set your payment terms

"Due on receipt" sounds firm but often gets ignored — most clients pay on a cycle. A specific window works better:

  • Net 7–14 for small projects and ongoing clients you trust.
  • Net 30 if the client is a larger company with an accounts-payable process.
  • A deposit upfront (often 30–50%) for new clients or larger first projects, with the balance invoiced on delivery.

Common mistakes that delay payment

  • Vague descriptions the client can't match to what was approved.
  • No due date, so the invoice drifts to the bottom of the pile.
  • Wrong contact — sending it to your project contact instead of whoever pays.
  • Missing payment details, forcing a back-and-forth before they can pay.
  • Reused or random invoice numbers that confuse their bookkeeping.

What to do about late payment

Most late payments are oversights, not refusals. Escalate calmly:

  1. Day after due: a short, friendly reminder with the invoice re-attached.
  2. One week late: a firmer note restating the amount and due date.
  3. Two weeks+: reference any late fee you agreed, and ask for a payment date.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to be a registered business to invoice?

In most countries you can invoice as a sole proprietor or individual using your own name. Check your local rules on tax registration thresholds, but you don't need a company to send an invoice.

Do I have to charge tax?

It depends on where you live and how much you earn. Many freelancers are below the threshold where sales tax/VAT applies. When in doubt, confirm with a local accountant — and show tax as a separate line when you do charge it.
