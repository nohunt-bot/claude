What to include on an invoice

A professional invoice isn't complicated, but leaving off the wrong detail is the fastest way to get paid late — or not at all. This is the complete checklist of what every invoice needs: the essentials that make it a valid record, plus the small extras that quietly get you paid faster.

The 10 essential elements

Every invoice you send should contain these, top to bottom. The first nine are the standard building blocks; miss one and a client's accounts team may bounce the invoice back and reset your payment clock.

Walking through each element

1. Label it clearly as an invoice

Put the word "Invoice" at the top. It sounds obvious, but an unlabeled document can get filed as a quote or estimate and never enter the payment queue. The label tells the client's accounts team exactly what to do with it.

2. A unique invoice number

Every invoice needs its own number so both you and the client can reference it later — "I'm paying invoice 1043" is a lot clearer than "the one from last Tuesday". Number them sequentially and never reuse a number. If you're not sure where to start, just begin at 1001. See how to invoice as a freelancer for a full numbering walkthrough.

3 & 4. Both parties' details

List your business name and contact info, and the client's legal name and billing address. Use the client's correct legal entity name — billing "John" when the company is "Acme Holdings Ltd" can hold up payment in a larger organization. If you charge sales tax or VAT, include your tax/registration number here too.

5 & 6. Invoice date and due date

The invoice date is when you issued it; the due date is when you expect to be paid. Always show a real calendar due date — "Due July 14" is unambiguous, whereas "Net 30" forces the client to do the math and gives them wiggle room. New to terms like Net 30? See invoice payment terms explained.

7. An itemized list of what you're charging for

Break the work into clear line items, each with a short description, quantity (hours or units), and rate. A client is far quicker to approve "Homepage design — 12 hrs @ $80" than a single lump sum with no breakdown. Itemizing also heads off "what is this for?" questions that delay payment.

8. Subtotal, tax, discount, and total

Show the math: the subtotal of all line items, any discount, any tax (sales tax or VAT) as its own line, and the final total due. Make the total the most prominent number on the page — it's the one thing the client is looking for.

9. How to pay

This is the element freelancers most often forget — and it's the one that costs you the most time. If the client has to email you back asking for your bank details, you've added days. Include your payment details directly: bank/account info, a payment link, or the methods you accept.

10. Payment terms and a note

State your terms (e.g. "Payment due within 15 days"), any late fee, and a one-line thank-you. A warm note — "Thanks for your business!" — costs nothing and keeps the relationship friendly even while you're asking to be paid.

What's legally required vs. what's just professional

The exact legal requirements vary by country, but in most places a basic invoice needs the seller and buyer details, a date, a description of goods or services, and the amount due. If you're registered for sales tax or VAT, a compliant tax invoice usually also needs your tax registration number, the tax amount shown separately, and sometimes specific wording. The rest of the checklist — clear numbering, a real due date, payment details, terms — isn't always legally mandatory, but it's what separates an invoice that gets paid in days from one that lingers.

If you're VAT-registered or invoicing across borders, check your local tax authority's rules for the exact required fields. When in doubt, include more detail rather than less.

A quick copy-checklist

Before you hit send, run down this list:

  • Labeled "Invoice" with a unique, sequential number
  • Your name, contact, and any tax ID
  • Client's legal name and billing address
  • Invoice date and a real due date
  • Itemized lines with descriptions, quantities, and rates
  • Subtotal, tax/discount, and a prominent total
  • Payment details — how, exactly, to pay you
  • Terms and a short thank-you note

Frequently asked questions

What are the essential things every invoice must include?

At minimum: the word "Invoice", a unique invoice number, your business name and contact, the client's name and address, the invoice date and due date, an itemized list of what you're charging for, the subtotal/tax/total, and how to pay. Those elements make it a valid, payable record.

Is an invoice number legally required?

A unique reference is expected on a professional invoice and is required on tax invoices in many jurisdictions. Even where it isn't strictly mandatory, always use a unique, sequential number — it's how both you and the client track and reference payment.

Do I need to include tax on my invoice?

Only if you're registered to charge it. If you collect sales tax or VAT, show it as a separate line with your tax registration number; if you're not registered, you simply don't add a tax line. Check your local tax authority's rules to be sure.

What should I put for payment terms?

State a clear due date and the window — for example "Payment due within 15 days (by July 14)". You can also note accepted payment methods and any late fee. See invoice payment terms explained for the common options.
