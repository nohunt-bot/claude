Net 30 payment terms, explained

"Net 30" is one of the most common phrases on a business invoice — and one of the most misread. Here's exactly what it means, how the 30 days are counted, how it stacks up against Net 15 and Net 60, and when it's the right term to put on your own invoices.

What does "Net 30" mean?

Net 30 means the full amount of the invoice is due 30 days after the invoice date. The word "net" refers to the net amount owed — the total after any discounts — and the number is the count of days the client has to pay it. So an invoice dated June 1 with Net 30 terms is due on July 1.

It's a credit arrangement, not a discount: you're letting the client hold the money for 30 days before paying. That's why larger companies like it, and why it's worth understanding before you agree to it.

How are the 30 days counted?

By default, the clock starts on the invoice date and counts 30 calendar days (including weekends and holidays). A few details trip people up:

  • Invoice date vs. delivery date. Unless you state otherwise, the 30 days run from the date on the invoice — not the day you finished the work or the day the client received the invoice.
  • Calendar days, not business days. Net 30 is 30 actual days. If you mean business days, you have to say so explicitly.
  • "End of month" variants. Some firms use "Net 30 EOM," meaning 30 days from the end of the month the invoice was issued. Spell it out to avoid disputes.

The cleanest way to remove all doubt is to print the actual due date on the invoice alongside the term — "Net 30 (due July 1, 2026)" — so nobody has to do the math.

Net 15 vs Net 30 vs Net 60

The number just changes the window. The trade-off is always the same: longer terms are friendlier to the client's cash flow and harder on yours.

TermDueTypical use
Due on receiptImmediatelySmall jobs, new or one-off clients, deposits.
Net 7 / Net 147–14 daysFreelancers and small projects with trusted clients.
Net 3030 daysThe standard for invoicing larger companies with an accounts-payable process.
Net 60 / Net 9060–90 daysLarge corporate or government clients; tough on a small supplier's cash flow.

As a rule of thumb: the bigger the client, the longer the terms they'll expect — and the more you may need to plan your own cash flow around the wait.

When should you use Net 30?

Net 30 is the right default when:

  • You're billing a company that pays on a cycle through an accounts-payable team — they likely can't pay faster anyway, so Net 30 matches their process.
  • The amount is large enough that the client wants a little time to review and approve it.
  • You have the cash-flow cushion to wait a month and still cover your own costs.

Lean shorter — Net 7 to Net 14, or a deposit upfront — when you're working with a new client, an individual, or a small project, or whenever waiting 30 days would strain your finances. You can always offer Net 30 as a courtesy to clients who've earned it.

Can you get paid faster on Net 30?

Yes — a common tactic is an early-payment discount, written as "2/10 Net 30." That means: the client may take a 2% discount if they pay within 10 days; otherwise the full amount is due in 30. It's a small price for getting cash three weeks sooner, and many companies will take it.

The other levers are simpler: put the due date in plain sight, send the invoice the moment the work is done (the clock starts on the invoice date, so don't delay), and follow up the day after it's due.

Frequently asked questions

Does Net 30 mean 30 business days or calendar days?

Calendar days, unless you explicitly say "business days." Net 30 is 30 actual days from the invoice date, weekends and holidays included.

When does the Net 30 clock start?

On the invoice date by default — not the delivery date or the date the client opens the invoice. If you want it to start from delivery or receipt, state that on the invoice.

Is Net 30 good for freelancers?

It's fine for larger business clients who pay on a monthly cycle, but for small projects and new clients a shorter term like Net 14 (or a deposit upfront) protects your cash flow better. See our guide to invoicing as a freelancer for how to set terms that actually get you paid.
