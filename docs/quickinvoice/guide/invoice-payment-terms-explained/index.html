Invoice payment terms explained

Payment terms are the part of your invoice that tells the client when and how to pay — "Net 30," "Due on receipt," "50% deposit," "2/10 Net 30." They look like jargon, but each one is a simple promise about timing. Get them right and you get paid faster; leave them vague and you end up chasing money. Here's what every common term actually means and how to choose the right ones for your invoices.

What "payment terms" really means

Payment terms are the agreed conditions under which you'll be paid. At minimum they answer one question: by when is this invoice due? Good terms also cover how the client can pay, what counts as the start of the clock, and what happens if they pay late or early. They belong in two places — the body of the invoice (a clear due date) and, ideally, your original agreement or quote so there are no surprises.

The single most important rule: state a specific due date, not just a term code. Writing "Net 30" is fine, but always pair it with the actual date ("Due 12 July 2026") so the client never has to do the math.

The most common payment terms, decoded

"Net" simply means "the full amount is due within this many days of the invoice date." The number is the count of days. Here are the terms you'll meet most often:

Due on receiptPay immediately, as soon as the invoice arrivesSmall jobs, new clients, one-off work
Net 7Pay within 7 days of the invoice dateFreelancers wanting fast cash flow
Net 15Pay within 15 daysA common, friendly middle ground
Net 30Pay within 30 daysThe default for many businesses & larger clients

Early-payment discounts and what "2/10 Net 30" means

One term confuses almost everyone: 2/10 Net 30. It's a carrot to get paid early. Read it as two parts: "2/10" means take a 2% discount if you pay within 10 days; "Net 30" means otherwise, the full amount is due within 30 days. So on a $1,000 invoice, a client who pays within 10 days pays $980; everyone else pays $1,000 by day 30.

Early-payment discounts can speed up your cash flow, but they cost you margin — that 2% comes straight off your total. Use them when slow payment hurts more than the small discount, and skip them when your clients already pay on time.

Deposits and milestone payments

For larger projects, you don't have to wait until the end to get paid. Two common structures:

  • Deposit upfront. Ask for 25–50% before you start. It secures your time and filters out clients who were never going to pay — see how to ask for a deposit as a freelancer.
  • Milestone billing. Split a big project into stages and invoice at each one (e.g. 30% at kickoff, 40% at draft, 30% on delivery). You get paid as you go and the client sees progress.

When you collect a deposit, send a receipt for it, and show the balance due on the final invoice. If you're billing the full cost up front for confirmation before any work, that's usually a proforma invoice.

How to choose the right terms

There's no single "correct" term — it depends on the client and the job. A quick way to decide:

  • New or unknown client? Lean shorter — Due on receipt, Net 7, or a deposit upfront.
  • Trusted repeat client? Net 15 or Net 30 keeps the relationship easy.
  • Large corporate client? They may insist on Net 30–60. Build that wait into your pricing and cash-flow plan.
  • Big project? Use a deposit plus milestones so you're never carrying the whole cost.
  • Cash flow tight? Offer a small early-payment discount, or simply ask for shorter terms.

Whatever you pick, agree it before you start work — ideally in your quote or contract — so the invoice just confirms what was already understood. If you're not sure what to charge in the first place, see how much to charge as a freelancer.

Getting paid on time once terms are set

Clear terms prevent most late payments, but not all. To keep things moving:

  1. Put the due date on the invoice — an explicit date, not just "Net 30."
  2. Spell out how to pay — bank details, payment link, accepted methods.
  3. Invoice promptly. The clock usually starts on the invoice date, so sending it late just delays your own payment.

Frequently asked questions

What does "Net 30" mean on an invoice?

Net 30 means the full invoice amount is due within 30 days of the invoice date. "Net" refers to the total owed, and the number is the count of days the client has to pay. Net 15 and Net 60 work the same way with 15 or 60 days.

What does "Due on receipt" mean?

Due on receipt means payment is expected immediately, as soon as the client receives the invoice. There's no grace period. It's common for small jobs, one-off work, or new clients where you want to be paid right away.

What does "2/10 Net 30" mean?

It's an early-payment discount. The client can take a 2% discount if they pay within 10 days; otherwise the full amount is due within 30 days. On a $1,000 invoice that's $980 if paid by day 10, or $1,000 by day 30.

What are the best payment terms for a freelancer?

For most freelancers, Net 15 is a good balance — fast enough for healthy cash flow, generous enough to feel fair. Use shorter terms (Due on receipt or a deposit) for new clients, and accept Net 30 for larger clients who require it. The key is to agree the terms before you start work.

When does the payment clock start?

Usually on the invoice date — so "Net 30" means 30 days from when you issued the invoice, not from when the work was done. That's why sending your invoice promptly matters: a late invoice delays your own payment.

Should I charge a late fee?

You can, but state it in your terms up front (for example, "1.5% per month on overdue balances"). A clearly agreed late fee gives clients a reason to pay on time and gives you leverage if they don't. Many freelancers start with a polite reminder before applying one.
